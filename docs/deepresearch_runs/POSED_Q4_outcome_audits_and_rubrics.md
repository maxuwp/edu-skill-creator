## The question

Course designers are told to write learning outcomes that are specific and measurable, and their
outcomes are then judged against that standard. I need to know what real published outcomes actually
look like when someone has counted, what instruments exist for judging an outcome *statement* rather
than a student's work, and whether any of it has been validated.

## Strand 1 — what real outcomes actually contain

Find published studies or institutional audits that **counted** features of real university learning
outcomes. Specifically: how often do outcomes include a condition (the circumstances of performance)
and a criterion (the standard of acceptable performance) — the two elements beyond the bare verb that
Mager's format calls for?

For each study: the population and sample size, the proportions found, a verbatim quote, and the full
URL. If the counting was done on a specific discipline or country, say which.

Verdict per study: `counted-and-reported`, `asserted-without-counting`, or `unverified`.

If no study has counted this, say so explicitly, list where you looked, and that is the answer to
this strand.

## Strand 2 — instruments for judging an outcome statement

Find **at least six independently published rubrics, checklists or scoring guides** — different
publishers, no two from the same institution — that score the **quality of a learning-outcome
statement itself**, not the quality of student work produced against it. Accreditation bodies,
assessment offices and published scholarship all count.

Table: `id | publisher | instrument title | year | the dimensions it scores | is it scored or
checklist-only | verbatim quote | host class | full URL`

## Strand 3 — has any of it been validated?

For the instruments found in Strand 2, and for outcome-quality judgement generally: is there any
published evidence of **reliability or validity**? Specifically, has anyone reported inter-rater
agreement among people scoring the same set of outcome statements with the same instrument?

Verdict: `reliability-reported`, `validity-reported`, `both`, `neither-found`. Give the statistic and
its source where one exists. **A finding of `neither-found` is a successful answer to this strand,
not a failure, provided you say where you looked.**

## Strand 4 — does outcome-statement quality predict anything?

Is there published empirical evidence linking the quality or form of an outcome *statement* to any
downstream result — student performance, assessment alignment, accreditation review results, or
instructor behaviour? Distinguish evidence about **alignment between outcomes and assessments**,
which is a different and better-studied claim, from evidence about **the wording of the outcome
statement itself**, which is the claim under test here.

Verdict: `evidence-found`, `only-alignment-evidence-found`, `no-evidence-found`, or `unverified`,
with quotes and full URLs.

## Evidence rules

Every claim carries a **verbatim quote** in quotation marks and the **full URL of the page hosting
those exact words**, written as plain text beginning with `https://` inside the cell. Not a canonical
topic page, not an organisation's home page, not a bare hostname.

Every cited row also carries a **host class**, which you assign yourself, from exactly this list:

- `publisher` — the publisher, journal, or issuing body's own domain
- `author` — the author's own site or their institution's page for that work
- `object-of-study` — a page that *is* the thing being surveyed, such as an institutional rubric in a
  census of institutional rubrics
- `rehost` — a genuine, complete copy of the real document on somebody else's domain
- `secondary` — a source quoting or summarising the document rather than being it

**If a row's host class is `rehost` or `secondary`, give the canonical publisher URL in the same cell,
even if it is paywalled or you could not open it.** Say which one you actually read. A labelled rehost
is usable; an unlabelled one is not.

If you cannot produce both a verbatim quote and the URL hosting it, the verdict is `unverified` and
you say what blocked you. A confident, plausible answer with no traceable quote is worse than an
honest `unverified` and will be discarded.

Blogs, SEO pages, content farms, aggregators and document resellers are pointers, not sources. Use
them to find the document, then cite the document.

For every row marked `unverified`, name the single specific next query or document you would try.

Report what the sources say. Do not tell me which instrument is best and do not recommend a course of
action.
